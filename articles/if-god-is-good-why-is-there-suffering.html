If God is good, why is there suffering?

By Pip Witheridge | Pastor, St Paul's Anglican, Carlingford

For many people, the problem of suffering is one of the biggest obstacles to belief in God. If God is good and powerful, why is there so much pain in the world?

For some, this is a philosophical question. For others, it is deeply personal, shaped by grief, illness, injustice, or loss. It can feel difficult to reconcile the reality of suffering with the idea of a loving God.

This is not a new question. People have wrestled with it for thousands of years. In fact, the Bible itself engages deeply with it.

The Bible and the reality of suffering

The Bible does not shy away from talking about suffering in connection to God. One of the clearest examples is the entire book of Job, found in the Old Testament. Job was a righteous man—he generally did the right thing—yet he experienced intense suffering that is not directly explained by anything he did wrong. The story does not offer a simple answer. Instead, it gives space for lament, honesty, and questions directed toward God.

Its overall message is not that suffering is always easy to explain, but that human beings are often not in a position to see the full picture of what God may be doing.

The Bible also makes clear that not all suffering is a direct result of personal wrongdoing. In John 9, Jesus is asked about a man who was born blind. The question is whether his blindness was caused by his own sin or his parents’ sin. Jesus rejects that assumption, saying that the situation is not a simple case of blame, but is bound up with deeper purposes that will be revealed through God’s work.

These stories encourage us not to rush to assign blame when people suffer. Instead, suffering calls for compassion and reflection.

Can suffering always be explained?

From a Christian perspective, suffering does not always have an explanation that is visible to us.

There are many situations in life where we simply cannot see any purpose or reason behind pain. That does not necessarily mean there is no reason—it may simply mean that our understanding is limited.

A helpful illustration is that of a needle in a haystack. If someone looks into a large haystack and cannot see a needle, that does not prove the needle is not there. In the same way, Christians would say that the inability to see a reason for suffering does not necessarily mean that no reason exists. It may mean that we do not have access to the full picture. Christians trust that God knows the full picture, and that despite our inability to perceive good reasons for all suffering, good reasons might nevertheless exist.

The cross and the goodness of God

For Christians, the most important part of this discussion is not an abstract explanation, but a person: Jesus Christ. At the centre of the Christian faith is the belief that God himself entered into human suffering in the life and death of Jesus. Christians believe that Jesus was not distant from pain, but experienced betrayal, injustice, physical suffering, and death.

This is why the cross is so significant. It shows a God who is not indifferent to suffering, but willing to step into it for the sake of the world.

Christians believe that Jesus’ death and resurrection are also the basis of hope that suffering will not have the final word.

Living with the question

This does not mean the question of suffering is easy to answer. Christians themselves continue to wrestle with it. The Bible often gives space for honest lament rather than quick solutions.

But Christians believe that suffering is not the end of the story.

The Christian hope is that God will one day renew the world, heal what is broken, and bring an end to pain, injustice, and death. The Bible describes this as a new creation—a restored world where suffering is no more.

An invitation

If you are wrestling with the question of suffering, you are not alone. It is a deeply human question.

Christianity does not ask you to ignore it or pretend it is easy. Instead, it invites you to bring your questions honestly, and to explore whether the life, death, and resurrection of Jesus might offer real hope in the midst of real suffering.
